# Project 1 — Edition A: IBM

**Prepared for:** Fund Investment Committee  
**Company:** International Business Machines Corporation (NYSE: IBM)  
**Valuation date:** September 1, 2026  
**Recommendation:** **INITIATE / BUY**

## Executive summary

I believe IBM is undervalued because the market reaction to the second-quarter update priced in a more permanent deterioration than the final 10-Q supports. On July 14, IBM pre-announced a weaker-than-expected quarter, and the shares fell 23% in morning trading. [4] The miss was real: revenue was below expectations, Infrastructure was weak, and IBM lowered its FY2026 constant-currency revenue-growth outlook to 4%–5%. [2][3] My disagreement is with the magnitude and permanence of the market’s conclusion—not with the fact that the quarter was disappointing.

The dislocation comes from the difference between what the market appears to have priced in and what IBM disclosed. Large deals failed to close in the final weeks of June as clients redirected budgets toward supply-constrained servers, storage, and memory. In the same quarter, Distributed Infrastructure revenue grew 37%. [3] This was a painful execution issue and a near-term headwind, but it is different from evidence that customers are abandoning IBM’s portfolio. I believe the selloff has created an opportunity to buy that distinction.

## Variant perception

The market treated one weak quarter as evidence that IBM’s software-led strategy is failing. I see a narrower problem: IBM was slow to adapt to a rapid change in client spending priorities and several large enterprise deals slipped out of the quarter. The company still needs to prove that this demand converts; the issue is not benign. But the 10-Q does not support treating the entire franchise as permanently impaired. [3]

The investment case is not that IBM deserves to trade like a high-growth, pure-play software company. It is that the market is underestimating the value of a larger recurring-software base, a gradually improving earnings mix, and the potential for free cash flow to support deleveraging. For the stock to work, the software and cash-flow components must become increasingly important—not every business line must be strong at once.

## Evidence supporting the thesis

- **FY2025 revenue:** $67.5bn, up from $62.8bn in FY2024. This establishes that IBM entered 2026 with renewed top-line growth. [1]
- **FY2025 Software revenue:** $30.0bn, up 10.6%. Software outgrew IBM overall, supporting the higher-quality revenue-mix argument. [1]
- **Software ARR:** $23.6bn, approximately $2bn higher year over year. The recurring software base is expanding. ARR is annualized recurring revenue rather than contracted backlog, so it is useful but not conclusive on its own. [1]
- **Q2 2026 Software revenue:** $7.8bn, up 5%. The latest quarter weakened the narrative but did not erase software growth; Red Hat grew 11% and Data grew 19%. [2]
- **Recurring software revenue:** Approximately 80% of Software revenue. The 10-Q states this recurring revenue delivered healthy growth in Q2, which is inconsistent with a broad collapse in the software franchise. [3]
- **H1 2026 free cash flow:** $4.8bn, flat year over year. Free cash flow remains meaningful; the catalyst is delivery on management’s expectation for roughly $1bn of full-year improvement. [2]

The weaker data should not be ignored. Q2 Infrastructure revenue declined 7%, and Transaction Processing revenue declined 8%. [2] These declines explain the market’s concern, but I think the share-price reaction gave insufficient weight to the continued growth in recurring software and to the likelihood that at least some of the delayed enterprise demand is timing-related.

## Management execution record

Management’s prior guidance is relevant because the current thesis depends on IBM converting its stated operational plan into cash flow. In early 2024, management expected about $12bn of free cash flow for the year; IBM ultimately reported $12.7bn. [6][8] In early 2025, management guided to at least 5% constant-currency revenue growth and about $13.5bn of free cash flow; IBM delivered 6.1% constant-currency revenue growth and $14.7bn of free cash flow. [7][1]

This is not a reason to assume that current FY2026 guidance will be met. The Q2 miss shows that management can be wrong about the timing of demand and needs to regain credibility. It does, however, show that IBM has recently delivered or exceeded its two core annual financial commitments—revenue growth and free cash flow. The burden of proof remains on management to repeat that execution in 2026.

The two prior 10-Ks provide useful context for that record. IBM reported $11.2bn of free cash flow in 2023 and $12.7bn in 2024, before reaching $14.7bn in 2025. [5][8][1] This progression supports the cash-generation component of the thesis, while the Q2 2026 cash-flow result makes clear that the trend cannot be extrapolated without scrutiny.

## Recent company developments

IBM announced an August 2026 partnership with OpenAI to jointly help enterprises deploy AI across core operations and complex workflows, with industry-specific initiatives in financial services, government, telecommunications, and retail. [9] Strategically, the announcement fits the thesis that IBM can participate in enterprise AI through deployment, integration, governance, and security—not only through owning the underlying model.

I do not treat this partnership as a valuation catalyst or as evidence of near-term revenue. It is a company announcement, not a disclosed contract-value or financial forecast. Its relevance is that it may strengthen IBM’s enterprise route to market and should be monitored for tangible bookings, client wins, or contribution to Software and Consulting growth.

### Balance sheet and capital allocation

Debt is the strongest argument against the stock. IBM ended Q2 2026 with $62.0bn of debt, including $13.0bn of IBM Financing debt, and $8.2bn of cash, restricted cash, and marketable securities. It also spent $10.5bn on acquisitions in the first half of the year. [2][3] The position therefore depends on disciplined capital allocation: acquisitions must produce durable software earnings and cash flow, while free cash flow must increasingly translate into net-debt reduction.

### Demand durability

The delayed enterprise deals may prove to be more than a timing issue. ARR does not disclose retention, churn, pricing, or the division between organic and acquired growth. The key question is whether the Q2 shortfall was temporary friction in client budgets or the first indication of a sustained slowdown in IBM’s software demand.

## Post-initiation scorecard

- **Software revenue and ARR:** Continued growth faster than IBM overall supports the thesis; material deceleration, especially in recurring revenue, challenges it.
- **Software margin and free cash flow:** Margin expansion and FY2026 free-cash-flow improvement support the thesis; growth that fails to convert into cash challenges it.
- **Delayed enterprise deals:** Conversion into Q3 and later-period revenue supports the thesis; further slippage or canceled demand challenges it.
- **Net debt:** Visible reduction after isolating IBM Financing debt supports the thesis; rising operating leverage or acquisition-driven debt growth challenges it.
- **Segment performance:** Strength in Red Hat and Data that offsets cyclical weakness supports the thesis; weakness broadening beyond Infrastructure and Transaction Processing challenges it.

Management expects 4%–5% constant-currency revenue growth and approximately $1bn of year-over-year free-cash-flow growth in FY2026. This is management guidance, not an independent forecast, but it provides a direct and measurable test for the thesis. [2]

## Final call

**INITIATE / BUY.**

I would change this call if the delayed enterprise deals fail to convert and software growth or free-cash-flow improvement stall while net debt remains elevated, because that would show the Q2 selloff reflected a lasting demand problem rather than a temporary dislocation.
